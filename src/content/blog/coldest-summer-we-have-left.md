I keep coming back to one line: this might be the coldest summer I have left.

Not because it will feel that way. Forecasters are talking about a [very strong El Niño](https://wmo.int/news/media-centre/strong-el-nino-expected-intensify) peaking somewhere between October and January, some models pushing sea surface temperatures past anything recorded since 1950. For South Africa that usually means hot and dry across the interior, dams drawn down, the maize belt praying for rain that doesn't arrive on schedule. It will not feel cold. It will feel like standing too close to an oven with the door open.

The line is about what comes after it, not what it is. Today is supposedly the coldest day of the rest of your life -- the joke climate people keep making, because the average keeps climbing and there's no real reason to expect this year to be the exception that reverses it. Apply that to a southern hemisphere summer and you get something uncomfortable: even this one, brutal as it might be, could turn out to be the mildest of the ones still coming.

I want to be honest about the limits of that line, because it's a slogan more than a law. Climate has noise in it. La Niña could show up in two years and knock the average back down for a season, and someone will point at that and say the doom was overstated. That's not the same as the trend reversing. The line about the coldest summer is true about the slope, not about any individual year, and I'd rather say that plainly than let the neat version do the emotional work for me.

What actually gets under my skin is smaller than global averages. It's dam levels on the news. It's remembering Cape Town counting down to Day Zero, queuing for twenty-five litres a day, and realising that was a preview screening, not the whole film. It's knowing Eskom's coal stations need water to cool themselves, so a bad drought doesn't just wreck a harvest, it can lean on the grid too -- water and power and food all plugged into the same failing system, and nobody voted for that arrangement, it's just how the country was built.

And it won't land evenly. It never does. Someone with a borehole and a generator gets a bad year. Someone in a settlement with one communal tap, or a farmworker paid by the day when there's a harvest to bring in, gets a catastrophe out of the same weather report. I don't have a tidy point to make about that, except that "climate change" as a phrase does a lot of work to sound like a shared experience when the exposure to it is not shared at all.

I've noticed I check rainfall outlooks the way I used to check load shedding schedules. A habit that used to be about surviving the day-to-day has stretched to cover the whole season, without me really deciding that. I don't love what that says about where my head is at. I'm not scared of one hot summer. I'm scared of getting used to being scared of them, one after another, until it stops registering as news and just becomes weather.

I don't have an ending for this that resolves anything. I'm not going to pretend naming the fear fixes it. But I'd rather look straight at the number -- a very strong El Niño, maybe the strongest on record, arriving on a baseline that was already rising -- than let it sit as background noise I've trained myself not to hear.
