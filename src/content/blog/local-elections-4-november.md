South Africa goes to the polls for local government elections on 4 November. I'm not going to pretend that's an exciting sentence. Municipal elections don't get the turnout or the attention that a national vote does, and I understand why -- there's no single face on the ballot, no one big story, just a list of wards and councillors most people couldn't name if you asked them. But this is the vote that decides whether your bins get collected, whether the water actually comes out of the tap, whether the potholes on your street get filled before your suspension gives out first. It's the closest government gets to your actual life.

I grew up watching load shedding schedules become a personality trait, watching municipalities collapse into administration, watching services that used to just work quietly stop working and nobody being held to account for it. Pretoria isn't the worst-run city in the country, not by a long way, but I've watched refuse collection get patchier, watched infrastructure age past the point where anyone seems to be planning for it, watched the same excuses get recycled election after election while the actual problem -- roads, water, electricity, the basic mechanics of a functioning city -- stays exactly where it was.

Five years is a long time to wait out a bad council. It's also a long time for the same people to keep doing what hasn't worked, on the assumption that nobody's really watching, or that habit and incumbency will carry them through regardless. Coalition politics since 2021 have made a lot of our metros unstable in ways that make it easy to point fingers everywhere and hold nobody responsible. That instability isn't a reason to disengage. If anything it's the opposite: contested councils are exactly where individual votes and individual pressure matter more, not less.

I don't think there's a single party or a single slogan that fixes a municipality. I do think the alternative to trying is guaranteed to be worse: another five years of the same drift, dressed up as continuity. The time for change is now, not because change is easy or because one election fixes decades of underinvestment, but because nothing changes without it. Council seats are won by turnout, ward by ward, and low turnout is exactly how the status quo protects itself.

So: check your registration, know your voting station, and show up on 4 November. It's the least glamorous vote you'll cast and the one most likely to actually touch your street.
